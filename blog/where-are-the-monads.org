#+TITLE: Where Are the Monads?
#+AUTHOR: Steve Downey
#+DATE: {{{modification-time(%Y-%m-%d, VC)}}}

#+begin_abstract
I have spent the last while implementing the recursion-schemes catalog, a
traversal library, a fold family, and a persistent sequence, all on one
typeclass mechanism, and I kept a ledger of what each algorithm actually
demands of its types. Monad came in last. This post is the claim that
organizes everything that follows: few algorithms require Monad in an
interesting way --- and the most interesting client C++ now has for it is
reflection.
#+end_abstract

# {{{TEASER_END}}}

* The Ledger

Twenty-odd recursion schemes. A traversal front door. A fold family. A
measured sequence. Every one generic, every one demanding some bundle of
named operations from its type arguments --- that is what a typeclass is,
a record of named operations, and the algorithms are constrained on the
record, not the type. So the ledger is easy to keep. What did each
algorithm need?

| Algorithm                              | Needs                        |
|----------------------------------------+------------------------------|
| ~fmap~, ~hoist~                        | Functor                      |
| ~length~, ~sum~, ~any_of~, ~fold_map~  | Foldable's one hook + Monoid |
| ~fold_fix~, ~unfold_fix~, ~refold~     | Functor                      |
| ~traverse~, ~transpose~                | Applicative                  |
| ~para~, ~zygo~, ~histo~ (fold extras)  | a Comonad --- the library's  |
| ~apo~, ~futu~, ~gana~ (build extras)   | a Monad --- the library's    |
| ~bind~, ~join~, ~kleisli~              | Monad. No caller above.      |

Monad appears twice. Both times the monad is scaffolding the library
supplies --- ~Identity~, ~either~, ~Free~ --- never a type you brought.
The generalized unfold is parameterized by a monad the same way the
generalized fold is parameterized by a comonad, and in both cases the
instances are the structural ones needed to state the scheme. Nothing in
the collection asks for a ~bind~ on /your/ type.

The bottom row is not an accident. The mechanism carries Monad without
strain --- the reference implementation ships the CRTP base, derives
~join~ and Kleisli composition from ~pure~ and ~bind~, and tests it. It
sits there as evidence. Nothing calls it.

* Chaining, Combining, and ∘

The ladder from Functor to Applicative to Monad is usually presented as
increasing power. It reads better as three answers to one question: what
is function composition, once context is involved?

Ordinary composition is ~g ∘ f~ --- feed the result forward. No context
anywhere.

Functor is composition preserved under a wrapper. ~fmap~ lifts one
function at a time, and the law is exactly that lifting respects
composition: ~fmap(g ∘ f) = fmap(g) ∘ fmap(f)~. Nothing combines,
nothing depends. You are still chaining plain functions, one level down.

Applicative is combining. ~invoke(f, ma, mb, mc)~ applies a pure n-ary
function to arguments that each carry context, and the contexts merge in
a way the instance fixes --- independent of any of the values. That
independence is the definition, not a limitation.

Monad is dependent chaining. The functions now have the shape
~A → M<B>~ --- they produce context along with the value --- and Kleisli
composition is the ∘ for that shape: given ~f : A → M<B>~ and
~g : B → M<C>~, ~kleisli(g, f) : A → M<C>~ runs ~f~, looks at the value,
and lets ~g~ decide what happens next. It is the context-sensitive
composition operator. And it is derived, not primitive --- ~pure~ and
~bind~ give it to you, which is exactly how the reference implementation
spells it. (It also wants an infix spelling. That is another paper's
problem.)

So the ledger above sorts by operator. Which grade of composition does
each algorithm need? Almost everything is answered by the first two
rungs. The dependent operator is the rare one.

* But C++ Is Full of Monads

The obvious objection: C++ is drowning in monads. ~optional::and_then~,
~expected::and_then~, ~flat_map~ on ranges, ~let_value~ on senders. All
of them ~bind~, each spelled as a member of its own class, none of them
generic. The committee has been shipping monads one at a time for a
decade; P0798 put ~and_then~ on ~optional~ by arguing exactly this.

However --- and this is the point the ledger keeps making --- the
/instances/ being everywhere is not the same as the /abstraction/ having
clients. An algorithm is a client of Monad when it is constrained on
~bind~ generically, the way ~transpose~ is constrained on Applicative.
And when you go looking for algorithms with that constraint, the standard
result is that most of the famous "monadic" ones are not. Haskell spent
its first two decades with ~mapM :: Monad m => ...~; McBride and Paterson
pointed out the constraint was overkill
([[https://www.staff.city.ac.uk/~ross/papers/Applicative.html][/Applicative Programming with Effects/]], JFP 2008), and today ~mapM~ is a
synonym for ~traverse~, which asks for Applicative. Traversal never lets
one element's effect depend on another's result, so it never needs
sequential composition. Independence is the common case. Monad is the
abstraction for the /dependent/ case --- the coalgebra that decides what
to build next by looking at what just happened --- and genuinely
dependent generic algorithms are rare. That is the whole thesis.

Rare is not none. The futumorphism builds several layers at a time
through the Free monad; an interpreter for a little effect language is
monadic through and through --- the reference tree contains one, a retry
program over a Freer monad, and it earns its keep. But these are
astronautics, honestly labeled. They prove the machinery composes. They
are not why you would standardize it.

* And Then the Language Grew One

So the papers this collection supports propose Applicative, Traversable,
Foldable, and the fold family, and propose no Monad. I know what the
first question in the room will be, and this post is the answer, filed
in advance. Deferred, not rejected: the typeclass design leads to a
consistent generic name for sequential composition eventually, and
per-type members --- ~and_then~, ~transform~, ~let_value~ --- are
precisely not that name. The slot stays open for the paper that needs
it.

Then, in March, Ben Deane pointed out that C++26 shipped an instance
([[https://www.elbeno.com/blog/?p=1813][/C++ Reflection: Another Monad/]]). Reflection's ~^^~ takes a type into
~std::meta::info~ --- that is ~pure~. ~substitute~ applies a type
function under the wrapper --- that is ~fmap~. ~extract<std::meta::info>~
flattens a reflection of a reflection --- that is ~join~. ~fmap~ then
~join~ is ~bind~, and Deane checks the laws. Type-function composition,
the metaprogramming C++ has done by exhausting template specialization
tricks for thirty years, is Kleisli composition in the reflection monad.

And this instance is different from ~optional~ in the way that matters:
there is no class to hang ~and_then~ off of. ~std::meta::info~ is one
opaque type for every reflected thing; the monad structure lives in free
functions the language gives you, and composing metaprograms means
composing those. A generic ~bind~ --- a generic Kleisli arrow --- finally
has a client that cannot solve its problem with a member function.
Whether that client is worth a paper is a question I intend to take
seriously rather than answer here.

* The Map

The claim above organizes a collection of posts, published together as
series. Each series stands alone; each post is supposed to be of some use
by itself --- a thing you can take away and apply without reading the
rest. The abstraction is the through-line, not the toll.

- *Transpose, Traverse, and Typeclass Objects* --- five posts. The loop
  you have written a dozen times, the two typeclasses under it, what it
  costs a type to opt in, and the prior art. Applicative territory,
  start to finish.
- *Recursion Schemes in C++26* --- the field guide, fourteen posts and an
  interlude. Functor territory at the start; comonads on the fold side
  and monads on the build side by the end, all structural, all supplied.
- The papers themselves --- traversal and transposition, algorithms for
  trees, the persistent measured sequence --- for when a post convinces
  you the library should exist.

Read whichever problem you have. The ledger will keep itself.
